Synaptic plasticity as short-term memory

Where a fact lives

Reading and breaking the synaptic state of Dragon Hatchling

A fact the model just read is held in a small, locatable set of synapses — not in its weights. Remove the entries it wrote and recall breaks. Remove twice as much state from anywhere else and it survives.

01

The model reads eight bindings, then one query

Every name–city pairing is drawn fresh for each sequence, so across training each name is paired with each city equally often. The weight-optimal answer to “which city follows this name” is uniform — whatever the model is about to do, it cannot be recalling from its parameters.

02

Every token writes a rank-1 outer product into ρ

BDH-GPU carries its state as ρt = (ρt−1 + LN(E yt−1) xtT) U — Eq. (8) of the Dragon Hatchling paper. That outer product is a Hebbian write: reading a token strengthens a specific set of connections. ρ is d × n here, 32 × 256, not neuron × neuron; the conceptual σ of Eq. (6) relates to it by σ ≈ Dyρ, rank at most d. What you are looking at is the state the trained model actually carries.

Sparsity is not imposed. The paper reports about 5% of the y vectors active in BDH-GPU’s state-space dynamics — activity varies with predictability rather than following a fixed budget.

03

Break it — and check the control

Removing state degrades any model. The result only means something against a control that removes the same or more state mass from somewhere else. Top-other takes the largest-magnitude entries outside this binding’s own write, so it always removes at least as much. If recall survives that while the targeted ablation breaks it, the effect cannot be explained by how much state was removed.

05

The same principle, one level up: BDH-CQ

BDH-CQ carries a contextual memory St that demonstrations update at inference, and solves the query by iterating in a latent workspace Hr — no parameters change at inference, and no chain of thought is written out. Its published interventions make the same point this page makes by ablation: with byte-identical held-out inputs, adding one demonstration at the test complexity moves depth-five nesting from 19/24 to 24/24, and recovers 13/24 from a 0/24 baseline at ordering length eight. What is in state decides — not what is in the weights.

Evidence labels, precisely. The 150M configuration reaches 29.5% pass@2 on public ARC-AGI-1 at a computed $0.00070 per task. That is a cost-efficiency result, not an accuracy win — GPT-5.6 Luna (Low) scores 34.2% on the same set. The audit reproducing 29.5% was conducted by co-authors, so it is not an external reproduction. Capability is structured, not scalar: rotation composes with relocation on 72/72, colour swap on 0/72. BDH-CQ’s internals are proprietary; nothing here reproduces them.

06

What we did not get

Three results we went looking for and could not stand behind. They are on the page because a submission that lists only its successes cannot be checked.

Decaynot demonstrated

Damping is the u<1 case of the paper’s own U. Across three regimes the curves never cross: u=1 matches or beats every damped model at every binding age, and below 0.95 overall recall collapses from 21.3% to 7.5%. The decay control was cut from this page rather than shipped as a slider whose effect we cannot show.

Capacity orderingnot established

A wider state should hold more bindings. Two of four widths never learned to hold even one — failing at zero competing bindings — so their curves measure undertraining, not capacity. Tripling their training budget improved both and converged neither.

Interferencewithdrawn

Hold the binding count fixed and vary only neutral filler, and recall still moves 50.4% → 100%. The load axis is confounded with sequence geometry, so any interference curve built on it is uninterpretable. This removed half of our original claim.
